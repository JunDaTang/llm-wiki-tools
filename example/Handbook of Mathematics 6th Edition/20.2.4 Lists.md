#### 20.2.4 Lists

##### 20.2.4.1 Notions

Lists are important tools in Mathematica for the manipulation of whole groups of quantities, which are important in higher-dimensional algebra and analysis.

A list is a collection of several objects into a new object. In the list, each object is distinguished only by its place in the list. The construction of a list is made either by the command

$$
{ \mathrm { L i s t } } [ a 1 , a 2 , a 3 , . . . ] { \mathrm { o r ~ b y } } \ \{ a 1 , a 2 , a 3 , . . . \}\tag{20.9}
$$

if the elements can be simply enumerated. To explain the work with lists, a particular list is used, denoted by l1:

$$
I n [ t { \cal I } ] : = l 1 = \mathrm { L i s t } [ a 1 , a 2 , a 3 , a 4 , a 5 , a 6 ] \longrightarrow { \cal O } u t [ t { \cal I } ] = \{ a 1 , a 2 , a 3 , a 4 , a 5 , a 6 \}\tag{20.10}
$$

Mathematica applies a short form to the output of the list: It is enclosed in curly braces.

##### 20.2.4.2 Nested Lists

The elements of lists can also be lists, so nested lists can be obtained. If entering, e.g., for the elements of the previous list l1 (20.10)

$$
I n [ 1 ] : = a 1 = \{ b 1 1 , b 1 2 , b 1 3 , b 1 4 , b 1 5 \}
$$

$$
I n [ 2 ] : = a 2 = \{ b 2 1 , b 2 2 , b 2 3 , b 2 4 , b 2 5 \}
$$

$$
I n [ 3 J : = a 3 = \{ b 3 1 , b 3 2 , b 3 3 , b 3 4 , b 3 5 \}
$$

and analogously for a4, a5 and a6, then because of (20.10) a nested list (an array) is obtained which is not shown here explicitly. One can refer to the j-th element of the i-th sublist with the command $\mathtt { P a r t } [ l , \ i , \ j ]$ . The expression $l [ [ i , j ] ]$ has the same result. In the above example (p. 1027), e.g.,

$$
I n [ 4 ] : = l 1 [ [ 3 \AA , 4 ] ] \quad \mathrm { y i e l d s } \quad O u t \ : [ 4 \AA ] = b 3 4
$$

Furthermore, $\mathrm { P a r t } [ l , \{ i 1 , i 2 \ldots \} , \{ j 1 , j 2 \ldots \} ]$ or $l [ [ \{ i 1 , i 2 , . . . \} , \{ j 1 , j 2 , . . . \} ] ]$ results in a list consisting of the elements numbered with $j 1 , j 2 .$ . from the lists numbered with $i { \bar { 1 } } , i 2 , \dots$

For the above example in 20.2.4.1, p. 1027

$$
I n [ t { \cal I } { \cal I } : = l 1 [ [ \{ 3 , 5 \} , \{ 2 , 3 , 4 \} ] ] \longrightarrow 0 u t [ t { \cal J } = \{ \{ b 3 2 , 6 3 3 , b 3 4 \} , \{ b 5 2 , b 5 3 , b 5 4 \} \} ]
$$

The idea of nesting lists is obvious from these examples. It is easy to create lists of three or higher dimensions, and it is easy to refer to the corresponding elements.

With Table, also higher-dimensional arrays can be created. With the expression

Table[f, i, i1, i2 , j, j1, j2 , . . .]

a higher-dimensional, multiple nested table is obtained, i.e., entering

```autohotkey
In[2] := Table[Binomial[i, j], i, 1, 7 , j, 0, i ]
```

the binomial coeficients are got up to degree 7:

```javascript
Out[2] = 1, 1 , 1, 2, 1 , 1, 3, 3, 1 , 1, 4, 6, 4, 1 ,
```

The operation Range produces a list of consecutive numbers or equally spaced numbers:

Range[n] yields the list 1, 2, . . . , n

Similarly, Range[n1, n2] and Range[n1, n2, dn] produce lists of numbers (arithmetic sequences) from n1 to n2 with step-size 1 or dn respectively. The command Array uses functions (as opposed to function values used by Table) to create lists. Array[Exp,5] yield $\{ e , \check { e } ^ { 2 } , e ^ { 3 } , e ^ { 4 } , e ^ { 5 } \}$
